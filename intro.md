# Introduction – RNA 3D Structure Prediction

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) has evolved in scientific understanding from a passive intermediary between DNA and protein into a central and versatile actor in cellular biology. Beyond messenger RNA, a vast and expanding universe of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) — transfer RNAs, ribosomal RNAs, small nuclear and small nucleolar RNAs, microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, ribozymes and riboswitches — regulates gene expression at the transcriptional, post-transcriptional and translational levels, catalyzes biochemical reactions, guides chemical modification of other RNAs, and senses metabolites [1,2]. Crucially, the biological function of most of these molecules is dictated not by sequence alone but by the three-dimensional (3D) architecture into which they fold. As with proteins, structure determines function: the catalytic core of a ribozyme, the ligand-binding pocket of a riboswitch, and the recognition surfaces that mediate RNA–protein and RNA–small-molecule interactions all depend on precise tertiary folding. This structure–function relationship also underpins the growing interest in RNA as a therapeutic target and modality; small molecules such as ribocil, which selectively modulates the bacterial flavin mononucleotide (FMN) riboswitch, illustrate the promise of structure-guided drug design against RNA [3,4].

Despite this importance, experimental determination of RNA 3D structures by X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, and cryo-electron microscopy remains laborious, in large part because of RNA's intrinsic structural flexibility, its highly charged and flexible backbone, and its weak, ion-dependent long-range tertiary interactions [5]. The consequence is a severe scarcity of experimental data: only around 6,000 RNA structures are deposited in the Protein Data Bank, far fewer than the roughly 190,000 deposited protein structures [5]. This structural gap has made computational prediction of RNA tertiary structure from sequence a long-standing and pressing goal.

Historically, computational RNA 3D structure prediction relied on two broad families of methods. Template-based (comparative) approaches build models by reusing homologous fragments or full structures already present in the Protein Data Bank, but they fail for novel folds lacking known homologs. De novo methods instead attempt to fold RNA from first principles: fragment-assembly approaches, most notably Rosetta's FARFAR2, assemble short structural fragments and refine them against an all-atom energy function [6], while coarse-grained physics- and knowledge-based simulation methods such as SimRNA sample conformational space using Monte Carlo methods guided by a statistical potential [7]. These approaches have been evaluated since 2011 through the community-wide, blind RNA-Puzzles experiment [8]. Their main limitations are the high computational cost of adequately sampling conformational space, a practical size ceiling of a few hundred nucleotides, and persistent inaccuracy in reproducing non-canonical base pairs and tertiary contacts.

The conceptual foundation for the deep-learning paradigm in structure prediction was laid by AlphaFold1, DeepMind's entry at CASP13 (2018), which outperformed competing groups largely on the strength of a single algorithmic idea [9]. Rather than assembling and screening large libraries of candidate fragments as prior methods did, AlphaFold1 trained a deep residual convolutional neural network to predict, from multiple sequence alignment-derived features, a probability distribution over the distance between every pair of residues and over the protein's backbone torsion angles. These predicted distributions were then combined into a single smooth, differentiable potential describing the whole protein chain, and the 3D structure itself — the atomic coordinates — was obtained by directly minimizing this potential through gradient descent, using the same backpropagation machinery used to train the network. This meant that folding was no longer treated as a discrete search over sampled conformations, but as a continuous optimization problem solvable by following the gradient of a learned energy landscape toward its minimum, a strategy that proved both more accurate and computationally far cheaper than exhaustive conformational sampling.

The 2021 successor, AlphaFold2, replaced this two-stage predict-then-optimize scheme with an end-to-end differentiable architecture (the Evoformer and structure module) trained jointly from sequence to coordinates, and in doing so achieved near-experimental accuracy in protein structure prediction [10]. Together, AlphaFold1 and AlphaFold2 catalyzed a wave of efforts to transfer deep learning to RNA. A first generation of RNA methods revived and adapted AlphaFold1's original predict-restraints-then-optimize-by-gradient-descent logic: a neural network predicts geometric restraints (inter-nucleotide distances, orientations and torsion angles) from multiple sequence alignments, and these restraints are assembled into a differentiable potential whose minimum is then found by gradient-based optimization rather than by conformational sampling. DeepFoldRNA [11], for instance, folds RNA by applying the L-BFGS gradient descent algorithm directly to a potential built from its predicted geometric restraints, and trRosettaRNA [5] follows a closely related restraint-then-refine strategy. DRfold [12] pushes this idea further by integrating end-to-end learning of local reference frames with deep geometric potentials within a single differentiable pipeline. A second, more AlphaFold2-like end-to-end paradigm predicts atomic coordinates directly from sequence: RhoFold+ [13] combines an RNA language model pre-trained on over 23 million non-coding RNA sequences with a folding module, NuFold [14] adapts the AlphaFold2 architecture with a flexible nucleobase-center representation, RoseTTAFoldNA [15] extends the three-track RoseTTAFold architecture to nucleic acids and protein–nucleic acid complexes, and, most recently, AlphaFold3 [16] employs a diffusion-based architecture capable of jointly predicting proteins, nucleic acids, and small molecules.

Notwithstanding these advances, deep learning has not yet delivered for RNA the transformative leap it produced for proteins. At CASP15, the first CASP round to formally assess RNA structure prediction, the top-ranked groups relied on knowledge-based energy potentials and manual conformational sampling rather than deep learning, and predictions from deep-learning approaches were found to be significantly less accurate than these expert, physics-based methods [17]. This pattern persisted at CASP16, where the assessors reported no notable increase in nucleic acid modeling accuracy from deep learning and where all top-performing groups were again human experts [18]. Systematic benchmarking corroborates these findings: while machine-learning methods generally outperform fragment-assembly methods, both categories perform poorly on RNAs lacking detectable homologs ("orphan" RNAs) and on non-canonical base-pairing interactions, which remain a shared weak point across all current methods [19]. The principal obstacles underlying these limitations are the scarcity of experimental RNA structures available for training relative to proteins, the frequent shallowness or absence of multiple sequence alignments for many RNA families, and the difficulty of capturing long-range tertiary motifs and conformational flexibility.

It is within this rapidly evolving but still maturing context — one in which deep learning has substantially accelerated and improved automated RNA structure prediction without yet matching the accuracy achieved for proteins — that the present work is situated.
